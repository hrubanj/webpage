Last year, I went through the books I read the year prior and highlighted the ones that impressed me the most.
I turned out to be a useful exercise. It helped me remember the books better and also to reflect on what I liked about them.
So, I decided to do it again this year. Here are my top 5 books of 2024.

### A Problem from Hell: America and the Age of Genocide by Samantha Power
Samantha Power documents how the US and the world have responded to genocides in the 20th century.
Disturbingly, the reactions were often late and lukewarm. The genocides did not occur out of the blue.
Usually, tensions were building up for years, but officials were reluctant to act. They irrationally believed that would-be
genocide perpetrators would respect international law, and that they would not engage in violence that would undermine their legitimacy.
Politicians were far too keen to take words of mass murderers at face value, and far too hesitant to risk their popular support to help the victims.

Power also writes about several bright exceptions–activists, politicians, journalists–that made it their lives' mission to prevent genocide, and
help the victims. Thanks to them, genocide is an international crime and several perpetrators have been convicted of it. The enforceability of anti-genocide
conventions will probably always be problematic, since many countries refuse to let international courts try their citizens.
But is it better to have an imperfect solution than no solution.

The book is a wake-up call to everyone who, knowingly or not, engages in wishful thinking. We should not discard
the worst-case scenario even if it contradicts our worldview that people are generally good and rational. Some are not.

### Determined: A Science of Life without Free Will by Robert Sapolsky
When does free will come into play? At what stage of making a movement, saying a word, or thinking a thought do
we make a decision that is completely independent of our environment and our past experiences? Sapolsky argues that
we never do.

The logic is hard to argue with: Any decision you make is a combination of your genetics, your upbringing and your environment.
You can always go up the chain of causality and find a reason why you made a decision.
Each event in the chain can have multiple causes and multiple effects, but the effect is always determined by the causes.
Even if you say: 'I'm going to eat this cookie because I want to', you can ask yourself why you want to eat the cookie.
And whether you decide to eat it or not, you have not decided freely.

The most intriguing part of the book is the implications of the lack of free will. Sapolsky argues that a significant share
of violence and crime is connected to traumatic brain injuries. At a risk of oversimplifying, people who have been hit in the head
and their brain has been damaged are more likely to commit violent crimes. This should not absolve them of responsibility, but it
can let us prevent future crimes. If that a person with traumatic brain injury is more likely to commit a crime,
we should try to do as much as we can to prevent and treat such injuries.

Should the existence or non-existence of free will guide your everyday decisions (pun intended)? I think this leads us to a Pascal's wager-like
situation. If free will doesn't exist, then you don't make any independent decisions. If it does exist, you should make decisions that make your live
more meaningful and fulfilling. The worst-case scenario is that you will live thinking that you are doing your best whilst having no actual choice.

### The Rise of Wolf 8: Witnessing the Triumph of Yellowstone's Underdog by Rick McIntyre
I like wolves. I'm yet to meet one in the wild, but I've read about them so much, and they never stop fascinating me.
Rick McIntyre amassed hundreds of hours of wolf observation in Yellowstone National Park. He reconstructed the lives of several wolf packs.

The dynamics of a wolf pack is a way more complex than you might imagine.
Leaders of some packs might be relatively small, but extremely courageous and smart. Some alpha pairs are chilled out and let their subordinates
show their talents. Some are aggressive and paranoid
Adult wolves play-fight with pups, and let them win. They also play-fight with each other, and let the weaker wolf win.
Wolf pups play games just to have fun or to learn how to hunt.
From time to time, a wolf is exiled from the pack or leaves voluntarily. He either decides to live alone, join another pack, or start his own.

If you like wolves, you will love this book.

### Africa is Not a Country by  by Dipo Faloyin
Dipo Faloyin tears down the idea of Africa as a monolithic grim place that needs saving from Westerners with Messiah complexes.

To say that Western public's perception of Africa is not nuanced would be an understatement. Media portray it as a place where wild
animals roam everywhere, uncivilized people live in primitive huts and make living by hunting said animals.
Evil and corrupt warlords fight each other for power, and there is no hope of establishing functioning states.

The reality is vastly different. Africa is a continent of many diverse countries, with millions of people living in cities,  and working in offices.
These countries have their unique culture, music, cuisine, and literature.
The mainstream narrative that Africans are not self-sufficient ignores that many African countries are independent, democratic, and peaceful.
Emotionally appealing ads with babies with inflated bellies and flies on their faces are designed to make donors feel good about themselves,
but many people living in Africa actually hate them. For obvious reasons–they are dehumanizing and perpetuate the idea that Africans are helpless savages.

Faloyin also discusses efforts to bring back stolen African art and artifacts from Western museums. Some people in the West argue
that Africans are not ready to take care of their own heritage while we, i.e. the West, can provide the best care possible. So, taking care of it on their behalf is the right thing to do.
Moreover, they claim that anyone can see the art in Western museums, so, what is the problem?
Ironically, none of these assertions is true–Faloyin documents numerous instances where African art is either hidden in museum basements,
and thus not available to the public, or it is stored in suboptimal conditions that damage the art.

Africa is Not a Country is a great book that can make you interested, entertained and furious often within a single page.

### Not the End of the World by Hannah Ritchie
Hannah Ritchie's book is refreshing. She writes about environment in a way that makes you hopeful and provides actionable advice
that you can actually follow.

Opportunistic politicians and unethical business interests often complicate environmental protection.
A lot of literature on the topic tends to be quite pessimistic, cataloguing our failures to implement even some basic measures.
Ritchie, however, tries not to give in to the disillusionment. She shows that real progress has been made. Often, governments
were forced to act not because people would suddenly start caring about the environment, but because people started demanding
cleaner cities as their living standards increased. She demonstrates that developed countries improved in several metrics in
envirnmental pollution in the last couple of years even if you account for emissions exported to developing countries.
She does not claim that everything is fine, and we should just sit on out hands, but rather points out that there is a way forward, and
that the there are degrees to which we can mess the environment up–not just binary 'apocalypse' or 'sunshine and rainbows'.

Ritchie translates the more objective approach to environmental protection also to individual actions. She compares who you
can do to reduce carbon emissions and what are the relative effects–turns out not flying and not owning a car save a lot of CO2
while using plastic straws and bags is not that big of a deal.

Informative and engaging. Highly recommended.
